The gap between the dashboard and the decision

On building things people praise and never open twice

Data Viz
Evaluation
When a dashboard changes what someone does, when a one-page memo beats it, and why every filter is a question you are handing back to the reader.
Published

August 26, 2026

You build the dashboard. It’s good. The layout works, the colours are consistent, the filters do the right thing, the load time is respectable. You demo it and people say nice things. Genuinely nice things, not polite ones.

Then you look at who’s opened it since.

I’ve had this experience more than once and I’ve stopped treating it as a design failure, because the design usually wasn’t the problem. The problem is that a dashboard answers a particular shape of question, and a lot of the questions people bring don’t have that shape.

What a dashboard is actually for

A dashboard is the right tool when three things are true.

The question recurs. Somebody needs to know this again next week, and the week after, with new data. If it’s asked once, you’re building infrastructure for a single answer.

The audience is stable and knows the domain. They can look at a number and tell whether it’s surprising. Someone encountering the data for the first time cannot do that, and a dashboard gives them no help learning.

The answer changes. If the number is the same every time anyone looks, the dashboard is a very expensive way to display a constant.

Most of the dashboards I’ve seen fail were failing the first or third test, and it wasn’t obvious at the time because the request sounded recurring. “We need visibility into enrolment” is a sentence about a standing need. Often what sits behind it is one person, with one question, in one meeting, that got generalised into a platform because a platform sounded more useful than an answer.

Three audiences who want different objects

Program staff need something operational. Their horizon is this week and their question is what to do about it. Which cohorts are behind on completion, who hasn’t been contacted, where’s the bottleneck right now. This audience genuinely benefits from filtering and drill-down, because they’re navigating to their own slice of the world and they know exactly which slice that is.

Funders need accountability. Their horizon is the reporting period and their question is whether the commitment was met. They want a stable, comparable, defensible set of figures with the caveats attached. This audience does not want to filter. Handing them an interactive tool means handing them the ability to construct numbers that aren’t the ones you’d stand behind, which is a strange thing to do on purpose.

Internal leadership needs comparison for allocation. Their horizon is the year and their question is where to put the next dollar. They want few numbers, across programs, with enough context to know which comparisons are fair. This is the audience most likely to be given a detailed operational dashboard and least likely to open it, because the granularity is noise relative to the decision.

Building one artefact for all three produces something that’s adequate for nobody. It’s the natural thing to do, because they all asked for “a dashboard” and building three feels wasteful. In practice the second and third audiences are usually better served by a page of prose with five numbers in it, which is much cheaper than the dashboard nobody uses.

Interactivity is a cost you hand to the reader

This is the reframing that changed how I build things.

Every filter, toggle and drill-down is a question you’ve decided not to answer. That’s sometimes exactly right. The program manager knows which region they care about and you don’t, so the filter is a genuine service.

But when you don’t know what the reader wants and you add a filter to cover it, you haven’t solved the problem. You’ve moved it. The reader now has to work out which combination of settings produces the view that answers their question, using a tool they use occasionally and a dataset they didn’t build. Most people, faced with that, look at the default view and leave.

The default view is the product. Everything else is optional depth. If the default doesn’t answer the most common question on its own, the dashboard doesn’t work, however good the interactions are.

I wrote a post about building a gradient legend for Power BI shape maps, and my own example in it was, as I said at the time, technically interactive and practically useless. Clicking a county filtered the legend to that county, so the minimum and maximum collapsed to the same value. The interaction worked perfectly. It just produced nothing worth looking at. I’ve since decided that was the most useful sentence in the post.

Annotation is the thing you’re actually selling

A number without context produces a meeting. A number with context produces a decision.

If completion rates dropped four points this quarter, the dashboard shows the drop. What happens next is that somebody notices, asks why, and a small investigation begins that ends three weeks later with a colleague remembering that the intake process changed in March.

If the dashboard had said the intake process changed in March, that entire cycle doesn’t happen.

This is the part that’s hard to build and easy to skip, because it can’t be computed. Somebody has to write it, keep writing it, and keep it current. A stale annotation is worse than none, so it’s an ongoing commitment rather than a feature you ship.

It’s also, in my experience, the single thing that most determines whether people keep coming back. A dashboard that explains its own movements becomes the place you go to understand what happened. One that only displays movements becomes a thing you check before a meeting so you’re not caught out.

When the memo wins

There’s one question, it has one answer, and the answer isn’t going to change much. Write it down. One page, the number, how you got it, what it doesn’t cover, what you’d do about it.

A memo has properties a dashboard can’t match. It carries its caveats permanently, in the same object as the finding. It can be read by someone who wasn’t in the meeting. It’s still comprehensible in two years. It can be forwarded without a licence, a login or an explanation of how to use it. And it forces you to commit to an interpretation, which is uncomfortable and exactly the point. A dashboard lets you present the data and let people draw their own conclusions, which sounds neutral and is often just deferral.

The reverse case is real too. Recurring operational questions asked by people who know the domain are genuinely badly served by memos, because you’d write the same one weekly and they’d stop reading it by week four.

The test I run first

Before I build anything now, I try to answer one question: what decision does this change, and who makes it?

If I can name the decision and the person, I build for that decision, and I let it shape the format rather than assuming the format. Sometimes that’s a dashboard. Fairly often it’s an email with three numbers in it, or a chart that gets pasted into a deck once a quarter.

If I can’t name either, that’s worth surfacing rather than working around. Sometimes the honest answer is that the request is about reassurance, or visibility, or having something to show, and those are real needs that just aren’t analytical ones. Building an elaborate analytical object to meet a non-analytical need is how you end up with something everyone praises and nobody opens.

I don’t think I’m arguing against dashboards. I like building them and I’ll keep building them. I’m arguing against building one as the default response to a question, before working out whether the question has the shape that a dashboard fits.
