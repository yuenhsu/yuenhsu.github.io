Deciding what “worked” means

The negotiation that happens before anyone collects anything

Evaluation
Turning a program goal into something measurable is a negotiation with people who have real stakes in where the bar sits, and it shapes the finding more than the analysis ever does.
Published

August 26, 2026

Someone asks you to evaluate a program. The goal, as written, is to increase employment outcomes for participants. It sounds measurable. There’s a verb, a direction, and a population.

It is not measurable. Not yet. And the gap between that sentence and something you can actually collect is where most of the real work lives, long before anyone opens a dataset.

I want to write about that gap, because it’s the part of evaluation that gets the least attention and determines the most. Analysis methods are well documented and mostly settled. Deciding what counts as success is neither, and it’s a negotiation with people who have genuine stakes in where the bar ends up.

Three questions hiding inside one goal

“Increase employment outcomes” contains at least three separate decisions that nobody has made yet.

What counts as employed? Full-time permanent work is employment. So is a casual shift. So is a contract that ends in eight weeks, self-employment that hasn’t turned a profit, and a role someone took because rent was due and left within a month. Every one of those is a defensible answer and they produce different numbers.

Increase relative to what? Compared to the participant’s own situation before the program? Compared to people who didn’t do the program? Compared to last year’s cohort? Compared to a target someone wrote into a funding agreement two years ago? These are not the same question and only one of them is about the program.

When do we look? At completion, when everyone is motivated and the program’s support is still in reach. Six months out. Two years out. Employment rates measured at different points tell genuinely different stories, and the choice of when to measure is often made by whoever set the reporting deadline rather than by anyone thinking about the program.

None of these have objectively correct answers. All of them have answers that make a program look better or worse. That’s the uncomfortable part: the measurement decision and the political decision are the same decision, and pretending otherwise doesn’t make it less true.

Outputs are not outcomes, and everybody knows this

The distinction is in every evaluation textbook. Outputs are what the program produced: sessions delivered, people enrolled, certificates issued. Outcomes are what changed for the participants.

Everybody knows this. Reports still lead with outputs, and the reason is not ignorance. Outputs are easy, they’re available immediately, they’re fully within the program’s control, and they always look like progress. Two hundred people completed the course. That is a real fact about a real thing that happened, and it’s genuinely useful for managing a program.

It just doesn’t answer whether the program worked.

The trap is that a long enough list of outputs starts to feel like an answer. Enrolment up, completion up, satisfaction scores high, waitlist growing. Every indicator points the same direction, and none of them are about whether participants are better off. A program can be operationally excellent and have no effect at all. Those are separate claims and they need separate evidence.

Where I’ve found this worth pushing on is the middle ground, the outputs that sound like outcomes. “Participants reported increased confidence” is an outcome in form and, depending on what the program is for, sometimes an output in substance. If confidence is genuinely the mechanism and you have reason to think it leads somewhere, measure it and say why. If it’s there because it’s the thing that reliably goes up on an exit survey, that’s worth being honest about.

Proxies drift towards the thing you measure

Every measure is a proxy for something you can’t observe directly. You can’t measure entrepreneurial capability, so you measure business survival at twelve months. You can’t measure whether someone is better off, so you measure income.

Proxies are unavoidable and mostly fine. The problem is what happens once people know which one you picked.

This is Goodhart’s law, usually stated as: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Campbell put a similar point more specifically about social programs, that the more a quantitative indicator is used for decision-making, the more it will distort the process it was meant to monitor.

In practice it’s less sinister than those formulations suggest. Nobody is falsifying anything. What happens is that a program, entirely reasonably, starts optimising for the number. If business survival at twelve months is the metric, the program gets better at supporting businesses through month twelve. It might do that by improving the coaching. It might also do it by recruiting participants whose businesses were going to survive anyway. Both raise the number. Only one is the program working.

The defence isn’t a cleverer proxy. It’s using more than one, choosing them so they’d be hard to move together without the underlying thing actually changing, and writing down at the start what pattern of results would make you doubt the program rather than only what would confirm it.

Selection is doing more work than you think

Most programs of this kind are voluntary. People opt in.

The people who seek out a workforce program, complete an application, show up to the sessions and stay to the end are systematically different from people who don’t. More motivated, more available, better resourced, better connected, or simply at a different point in their circumstances. That difference existed before the program touched them.

So when a completed cohort has better outcomes than the general population, some of that gap is the program and some of it is who walked through the door. Without a comparison that accounts for it, you can’t say how much is which, and the honest reporting of a single-group before-and-after design is that you measured change, not effect.

Attribution language matters here and it’s worth being deliberate. There’s a real difference between “participants’ employment improved” and “the program improved participants’ employment”, and the first is often all your design supports. Contribution language, where you make a reasoned case that the program plausibly contributed alongside other factors, is not a weaker claim. It’s a more accurate one.

Who gets to decide where the bar sits

This is the part that isn’t in the textbooks.

The funder wants evidence of impact, because they have to justify the spend to someone else. The program staff want a measure that reflects the work they actually do, and they’ll tell you, correctly, that the headline metric misses most of it. Leadership wants something comparable across a portfolio of programs that aren’t really comparable. Participants, if anybody asks them, often define success in terms nobody has written down.

Everyone is being reasonable. They want different things.

What I’ve settled on is doing this conversation deliberately and early, in a room, rather than letting it happen by default through whoever drafts the reporting template. Because it does happen either way. A measurement framework assembled quietly by one person under deadline still encodes a position on all of the questions above. It just encodes it invisibly, and then it’s very hard to reopen, because by then there’s a year of data collected against it and changing the definition means losing the comparison.

The version of this conversation that works, in my experience, is not asking people what to measure. It’s asking what they’d expect to see if the program were working, and separately, what they’d expect to see if it weren’t. The second question is the useful one. It’s much more specific, people find it harder to answer with platitudes, and disagreements surface immediately rather than in eighteen months.

Sometimes the answer is that you can’t know

The hardest thing to say to a stakeholder is that the design won’t answer the question they’re asking.

If there’s no comparison group and no plausible way to construct one, you cannot isolate the program’s effect, and no amount of statistical work performed afterwards will conjure one into existence. If the outcome takes three years to appear and the reporting cycle is annual, you’re measuring something else. If the sample is forty people, you can describe them well and you cannot detect a modest effect.

Saying this early is unpopular and much better than the alternative, which is producing a number that looks like an answer and gets quoted for years. Once a figure is in a slide deck it stops carrying its caveats. It gets repeated, rounded, and eventually attributed to a source that no longer resembles what you actually did.

The constructive version is to say what the design can answer, and what it would take to answer the other thing. Often that’s a smaller, sharper question that’s genuinely worth having. “We can’t tell you the program’s effect on employment. We can tell you that of the people who completed it, this is what happened to them, this is how that compares to their own baseline, and here’s what we’d need to say more.”

What I do now

Roughly, and not always in this order:

Start from the decision. Ask what someone will do differently depending on the answer. If nobody can name a decision, the measure is for reassurance, and it’s worth knowing that before you build a collection system around it.

Write the theory of change down, even roughly. Not for its own sake, but because the exercise forces the question of what’s supposed to cause what. Measures then attach to specific links in that chain rather than floating free, and gaps become visible as gaps.

Ask what failure looks like, in advance, in specific terms. Written down before data exists.

Pick a small number of measures that would be hard to move together by accident.

Write the definition down at the level of detail where two people applying it independently would code the same case the same way. “Employed” is not a definition. It’s a category name.

Then, and this is the part that matters most for anything running longer than a year, treat that written definition as something that can only be changed deliberately, with a record of what changed and when. Which is a whole separate problem, and one I’ll come back to.

None of this is difficult. It’s all just slower than starting the analysis, and it happens at a point in the project when everyone would rather be doing something that looks like progress.
