What breaks when you measure the same thing for five years

Every change is individually reasonable and collectively fatal

Evaluation
Data
How a longitudinal measure quietly stops being comparable to itself, and the governance that prevents it. Mostly a problem of who is allowed to change what, not of data cleaning.
Published

August 26, 2026

Nobody sets out to break a time series. That’s what makes it happen.

The changes that destroy comparability are, one at a time, obviously good ideas. A confusing question gets reworded. A response option that everyone complained about gets added. The intake form moves from paper to a web form because the paper was ridiculous. A funder updates its reporting categories. Someone notices a definition was too narrow and widens it.

Each of these improves the instrument. Together they mean year five isn’t measuring the same thing as year one, and usually nobody notices until someone asks why the trend line has a step in it.

This is a governance problem wearing a data cleaning costume. You can’t fix it downstream, because by the time it reaches you the information you’d need is gone.

Four ways a series quietly stops being itself

Wording. Survey methodologists have documented this for decades: small changes in question wording produce meaningful shifts in response distributions. Not because respondents are careless, but because the question is genuinely different. “Are you currently employed?” and “Are you currently working for pay?” catch different edges. The second picks up informal and casual work the first sometimes misses. Both are fine questions. Swapping one for the other mid-series introduces a change in the numbers that has nothing to do with the world changing.

Response options. Adding a category redistributes everything. If your employment status question gains a “self-employed” option in year three, self-employed respondents who previously selected “employed” now don’t. Employment appears to fall. It didn’t. You gave people somewhere else to go. The same applies to removing an option, reordering a list, or changing “prefer not to say” from present to absent.

Mode. How the question is asked changes the answer. Paper, web, phone and in-person interviews produce different response patterns on the same items, particularly for anything sensitive, where the presence of a human being makes people answer differently. Mode effects are well established and mostly invisible in the resulting dataset, because the dataset records the answer, not the circumstances.

Response rates shift too, which changes who is in your sample. A web form is easier for some people and a barrier for others, and those groups aren’t randomly distributed.

Definition. The most dangerous one, because it leaves no trace in the instrument at all. The question is identical. The form is identical. What changed is how the person entering the data decides what counts. A new staff member is trained slightly differently, or the guidance gets clarified in a way that reads as obvious but shifts a boundary case from one bucket to another. Nothing in the data indicates that anything happened.

The column that will never fill

A structural version of the same problem, and the one that hurts longest.

You add a field in year three because it’s useful and you should have had it from the start. From that point on, it’s populated. Years one and two are null, permanently, because those participants are gone and nobody is going to reconstruct their answers.

This is unavoidable and mostly fine, as long as everyone downstream knows that the nulls mean “not collected yet” rather than “answered nothing”. Those are radically different and they look identical in a database.

Where it turns bad is when someone runs an analysis across the whole history, drops nulls without thinking about it, and produces a result that silently excludes the first two years. The output is a valid number computed from a biased subset, it carries no warning, and it looks exactly like a number computed from everything.

The related failure is retroactive fixing. Someone widens a definition and, being helpful, goes back and recodes historical records to match. Now the historical data is neither the old definition nor an accurate record of what was originally captured, and the change is invisible because it’s been applied uniformly. If anyone published a figure from the old data, it no longer reproduces, and nobody knows why.

Don’t rewrite history. Add a column, keep both, document which is which.

What statistical agencies do that programs don’t

National statistical offices have solved this, or at least made peace with it, and their practices are worth stealing.

They publish breaks in series. When a methodology changes, the published output says so, explicitly, at the point where the change occurs. Data users see the discontinuity rather than discovering it. The trend line has a marked gap rather than a step that looks like a finding.

They run instruments in parallel. When switching to a new question or a new mode, both versions run alongside each other for a period, on the same population. That overlap is what lets you estimate the size of the change caused by the instrument, separate from the change caused by the world. It costs real money and it is the only way to actually know.

They maintain a codebook that versions every variable. Not a data dictionary describing current state, a document recording what each variable meant in each period, with dates.

Programs typically do none of this, and the reason isn’t incompetence. Statistical agencies have measurement as their product. For a program, measurement is overhead attached to the actual work of running the program, and the person who’d have to insist on a parallel run is asking for budget to collect data twice in order to prevent a problem that hasn’t happened yet.

That argument is hard to win. It’s easier if you’ve been through it once, which is a bleak thing to say but true.

The governance question

The useful reframing is that this isn’t about data. It’s about who is allowed to change an instrument, and what has to happen when they do.

In most setups the answer is that anybody can change it, informally, and nothing has to happen. The form is owned by whoever administers it. A program manager notices a question isn’t working, asks the person who maintains the form, and it’s updated by Thursday. Everyone involved has behaved sensibly. The series is now broken and there is no record.

What works better, and it doesn’t need to be heavy:

A named owner for each instrument. One person who has to be told. Not to create a bottleneck, but so there’s someone whose job includes noticing the implications.

A change record. What changed, when, why, and what the effect on comparability is expected to be. Three sentences. The “why” matters more than people expect, because in three years the change will look arbitrary without it and someone will consider changing it back.

A standing question at the point of change. Does this break comparison with prior years? If yes, is that acceptable, and what are we doing about it? Sometimes the answer is that it’s fine, the item was never used in trend reporting. Sometimes it means running both for a cycle. Either way the question got asked.

Version the definition, not just the form. The instrument is the questions plus the guidance on how to interpret them. If the guidance changes, the instrument changed, even if the form is byte-identical.

Break it on purpose sometimes

The failure mode at the other end is preserving a bad measure forever because changing it would break the series.

This is real and I’ve watched it happen. A question everyone acknowledges is poorly worded stays in the survey for years because the trend is the only trend anyone has. The cost of the break is visible and immediate. The cost of continuing to collect a measure nobody trusts is diffuse and easy to defer.

At some point the honest move is to change it, mark the break clearly, run both for a cycle if you can afford it, and accept that the trend restarts. A five-year series of a measure that doesn’t capture what you care about isn’t an asset. Continuity is only worth protecting when the thing being continued is worth having.

The judgement call is whether the old measure is bad enough to justify losing the comparison, and I don’t think there’s a rule for it. What helps is that it’s a decision someone made deliberately, with a record, rather than something that happened.

What I’d tell past me

Write down what each variable means, at the level of detail where two people would code the same case identically, and date it.

When something changes, record it in the same place, and never edit history to match the present.

Keep raw captured values and derived values separate, so a definition change means a new derivation rather than a rewrite.

Assume that in three years the only person who remembers why any of this is the way it is will be you, and possibly not even you.

None of that requires tooling. A markdown file in version control does it. The hard part was never the format.
